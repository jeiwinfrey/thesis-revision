LIGTAS-CASCADE

Utilizing Isolation Forest and XGBoost Algorithms in Forecasting Anomalous Trip Behavior as a Commuter Safety Indicator

A staged model that turns bare latitude, longitude and timestamps into a forward-looking Safety Index for a commuter's next 60 seconds. An Isolation Forest defines what “abnormal for this place and time” means; XGBoost learns to forecast it; an explanation layer says why.

Read this first. The Test Route dataset is synthetic and has no safety labels. “Safety” in this prototype therefore means forward anomalous-behaviour risk: the chance that the vehicle’s motion in the next 60 s is abnormal for that road position and time of day. It is a proxy. It becomes a true commuter-safety model only after incident or perceived-safety labels are added (see Columns to add).

    2 · The model

    Four stages. Stages 1 and 2 learn; stage 0 prepares the features and stage 3 turns the forecast into something a commuter can read.

    STAGE 0Kinematic and map-matched features

    From each 10-s fix: speed, acceleration, jerk, turn rate, dwell time (seconds stationary), 60-s rolling statistics, and the vehicle’s along-route position s and cross-track offset d from the route polyline. Time features: local hour, day of week, elapsed trip time. Only past and present samples are used.

    STAGE 1Context-conditioned Isolation Forest (the “teacher”)

    Instead of asking “is this speed unusual?”, it asks “is this speed unusual here, at this time of day?”. A residual v − median(v | 100 m bin, direction, am/pm) is added to the forest’s inputs, so a stop at a traffic light is normal and the same stop mid-block is not. The top 10 % of training scores define an anomalous moment.

    STAGE 2Forward-risk XGBoost (the “student”)

    Target: will any of the next 6 samples (60 s) be an anomalous moment? Inputs: Stage-0 features, the Stage-1 score, and a leave-own-trip-out segment memory (smoothed historical anomaly rate of that 100 m bin). This turns detection into prediction.

    STAGE 3Safety Index and explanation

    Safety Index = 100 × (1 − p), where p is the forecast probability. Per-prediction reasons come from XGBoost’s built-in tree-SHAP contributions. A Platt-scaling step is included but only used when enough validation data exists (see Results).

    How this differs from “Isolation Forest, then XGBoost on the same features”

    1. The forest is contextual. It scores deviation from the local norm, not from the global distribution.
    2. XGBoost forecasts; it does not re-detect. The label sits in the future (t+10 s … t+60 s), so the supervised model must learn what precedes anomalies.
    3. Validation is blocked by trip and time. Train on earlier trips, test on later ones, so a trip never straddles the split.
    4. Every component carries an ablation. The results below show which parts earned their place on this dataset.

    5 · Strengthening round (v2)

    Second pass after a literature search and review of the Edwards & Hosein (2025) deviation scheme. The evidence is now an injected-anomaly benchmark with exact ground truth, not just the model’s own label.

    What changed. New features: history-based and route-polyline deviation with the Edwards & Hosein bands, heading deviation, sustained off-route time, excess and unexpected dwell. New safeguards: a persistence baseline, a rule-based label channel independent of the forest, a rotated Isolation Forest, threshold and seed sensitivity, and a cold-start curve. All numbers below come from pipeline2.py and six rolling-origin folds (train on earlier trips, test on the next six).

      6.2 Ordinal concern level: Normal, Minor, Major, Critical

      The level of any moment is the worst of three signals: statistical unusualness (forest score above the 90th, 97.5th or 99.5th training percentile), route deviation (Edwards & Hosein bands), and excess dwell (30, 90 or 180 s). The model forecasts the worst level in the next 60 s using three cumulative XGBoost models, “level ≥ Minor”, “≥ Major”, “≥ Critical” (Frank & Hall, 2001), turned into one level using thresholds tuned on the validation trips.

      6.3 Calibration

      Calibration asks whether “60 % risk” means about 60 % of such moments really were followed by concern. Each calibrator was fitted only on earlier folds and tested on the next (Guo et al., 2017, for the ECE measure; Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana, 2005, for the methods).

      6.4 Uncertainty

      Two tools. (1) The spread of five XGBoost models trained on resampled trips. (2) Conformal prediction, which outputs a set of possible answers: “normal likely”, “concern likely”, or “uncertain” (both) so that the true answer is inside the set at least a chosen share of the time (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2021). An adaptive version (Gibbs & Candès, 2021) retunes itself as data drifts.

      7 · Fourth round (v4): speed changes and false alarms

      Run by evaluate.py, which now also serves as the verification tool. Every number here is from held-out trips and synthetic planted events.

      Read before using these numbers. The big gains below come from a supervised detector trained on planted events. It was tested on magnitudes and event kinds it never saw, but the planted events and the test events come from the same generator, so this shows the idea can work, not that it transfers to real riders. It must be re-tested on real trips.

      7.1 What was added

      • Speed features: sudden-ratio of recent to earlier moving speed, a moving-only speed z-score, acceleration sign changes, oscillation of route distance, reversal count. Added to the forest as “forest + speed features”.
      • Supervised detector: XGBoost trained on clean training fixes versus fixes inside events planted in training trips only (detours, a 90 s stop, speed ×1.5 and ×0.5). Position and time-of-day features are excluded so it cannot memorise the route.
      • Hybrid: alert if the forest or the supervised detector fires. The forest stays the catch-all for unknown unknowns; the supervised detector covers known families.
      • Budgeted alert policy: the threshold is chosen on validation trips to meet a false-alarm budget (episodes per clean trip), and an alert needs two consecutive fixes.

      8 · What this does not show

      The honest limits, so the thesis does not over-claim.

      • No real safety signal. The label is derived from the Isolation Forest itself, so XGBoost is largely learning to reproduce a rule. High PR-AUC means the anomaly is predictable from preceding motion; it does not mean the anomaly is dangerous.
      • The data is synthetic. The generator places stops, U-turns and traffic lights at fixed route points. Persistent “hotspots” (for example the 900–1,100 m zone) are generator parameters, not risk discovered in the world.
      • 10-second sampling hides harsh events. Acceleration never leaves roughly −1.0 to +0.9 m/s², so the hard-braking and jerk thresholds used in bus-safety studies cannot trigger. Those studies sampled faster or used accelerometers.
      • Small, single-route, single-vehicle sample. 60 trips, one tricycle route, daytime only (07:00–09:59 and 13:00–17:59 local). Fold-to-fold PR-AUC varies by about ±0.06–0.09, so differences under about 0.05 between XGBoost variants are noise.
      • The Isolation Forest score and segment memory did not add measurable skill as extra XGBoost inputs here. The kinematic features already carry the same information. The forest’s value in this design is defining the label, not boosting the features.
      • Calibration needs more validation data. With 9 validation trips, Platt scaling made calibration worse. It should be re-tested once real labels and more trips exist.
      • Speed anomalies are caught only by the supervised detector, and only on synthetic events. The unsupervised forest still scores about chance-plus on them (AUC about 0.65). The supervised detector reaches about 0.86, but it learned from events planted by the same generator. Do not claim the model detects reckless speeding until it is tested on real trips.
      • The forest is not better than other unsupervised detectors. Local Outlier Factor scores the same or slightly higher on average. The case for the forest is that it matches them, beats fixed rules on unseen event types, and is cheap to run.
      • False alarms are about one episode per six-minute clean trip at the fixed Major level, and need a budget below about one per three trips to be tolerable. Real trips will differ.
      • Early warning is weak and only where a run-up exists. Sudden planted events were not anticipated at all. When a stop or detour built up gradually, the 60 s before it scored about 0.6–0.7 AUC (chance 0.5). Forecast skill on the forest label shows predictable structure in normal trips, not warning of dangerous events.
      • Fusion did not beat the forest alone. Max-rank fusion of the forest, history deviation and excess dwell lost to the forest on most injected types and degraded at low trip counts.
      • Night is absent. A time-of-day risk effect (documented in survey work) cannot be learned from daytime-only trips.

      9 · Columns that would make this a real safety model

      None are required to run the prototype. Ranked by how much they change the thesis claim.

      PriorityColumn / fileWhy
      1incident_flag (and incident_type) per trip or per timestampGives XGBoost a real supervised target (near-miss, crash, harassment, theft) so the forest becomes a feature or auditor instead of the label maker. This is the single change that turns the proxy into commuter safety.
      2perceived_safety (1–5) per trip, from rider surveyCheaper than incident data and directly measures commuter feeling; usable as an ordinal target.
      3passenger_count or occupancy per timestampThe manifest hints at passenger minimums. Crowding and lone-rider status change exposure.
      3rider_profile (e.g. sex, age band; anonymised)The Philippine literature reports gendered risk, so a model can be checked for fairness across groups.
      4Night and wet-season trips; weather, lightingCurrent data is daytime only. Timestamps can derive lighting, but trips must exist at night.
      4More routes and vehicle types (jeepney, UV Express, bus)One route cannot test spatial generalisation. Needed for leave-one-route-out validation.
      5Faster sampling (1–2 s) or accelerometerMakes harsh-braking and jerk features meaningful.
